The box & pan brake is the manual sibling of the [Titan 25T](/forming/langmuir-titan-25t/). Its clamping bar is split into removable **fingers**, which is the whole trick: you can pull fingers out to leave gaps for flanges you've already bent, so you can fold all four sides of a box or pan without the last bend colliding with the machine. Use it for light-gauge brackets, enclosures, bellypans, and electronics trays where you don't need CNC-repeatable angles.

## Safety

<SafetyWarning type="warning" title="Respect the capacity plate">
A finger brake is a light-duty machine. Its rated capacity (stamped on the machine) assumes full-width mild steel — and aluminum ratings don't transfer to steel. Never bend material heavier than the plate rating, never bend rod, wire, or strap in it, and never "cheat" a heavy bend in a short section without asking a mentor first. Overloading bows the clamping bar and ruins the brake for everyone.
</SafetyWarning>

- Fingers pinch. Keep hands clear of the clamping bar when locking it, and clear of the apron when it swings up.
- The counterweight and apron handles move fast — control the apron on the way down, don't let it drop.
- Deburr sheared edges before handling. No gloves needed here, but see [PPE & the glove policy](/safety/gloves-and-ppe/) for the lab-wide rules.
- Hardened steel, stainless above the derated capacity, and polycarbonate (it cracks cold) all need mentor approval.

## Capacity Rules of Thumb

- Full-width bends: stay at or under the plate rating for mild steel; aluminum of the same thickness bends much easier.
- Stainless: derate roughly two gauge sizes from the mild-steel rating.
- Short bends spread less load across the fingers than you'd think — the capacity limit is about the clamp and apron, so the plate rating still applies.
- 6061-T6 aluminum cracks on tight radii. Bend it across the grain where possible and ask a mentor if the flange matters structurally. 5052 is the forgiving aluminum — prefer it for bent parts.

## Setting Up Fingers for a Box

1. Sketch your bend sequence first (next section). Count which bends need finger gaps.
2. For a box, set the fingers so their combined width matches the **inside dimension of the box, minus a small clearance** (about one material thickness per side), centered on the bed.
3. Loosen the finger clamp screws, slide or remove fingers, and re-tighten every screw — a loose finger creases the sheet.
4. Check the finger edges are aligned flush with each other. A proud finger prints a line into your part.

## Bend Sequence Planning

The whole game with boxes is order of operations — each bend must clear the fingers and the apron:

1. Bend the **two opposite sides first** (the long sides, usually). These are plain full-width bends.
2. For the remaining two bends, the already-bent flanges must swing up **into the gaps** where you removed fingers.
3. If a flange traps the part no matter the order, your box is too deep for its footprint — redesign, or split the part and rivet it.
4. Fold a paper or cardboard model when in doubt. Thirty seconds of paper saves a ruined part.

## Bending Technique

1. Mark the bend line and account for **bend allowance** — the flat pattern from your CAD sheet-metal tools already includes it; trust that over ruler-and-guess.
2. Slide the sheet under the fingers, align the bend line with the leading edge of the fingers, and lock the clamp handle firmly.
3. Raise the apron smoothly to your target angle, then a few degrees past it — sheet **springs back** a couple of degrees when released.
4. Release, check the angle with a protractor or against a known-square reference, and touch up if needed.
5. Repeat per your sequence. Same operator, same technique for every bend on a part — consistency is accuracy on a manual brake.

## When to Use the Titan Instead

Take the part to the [Titan 25T](/forming/langmuir-titan-25t/) when you need any of: repeatable angles across many parts, material heavier than this brake's plate rating, precise flange lengths off the CNC backgauge, or bends in 1/8 in plate. The box & pan brake wins when you need finger gaps for closed boxes — the Titan's continuous die can't do the fourth wall of a deep box.
