The Grizzly mill/drill — the machine labeled **"Mill"** on the machine-shop bench, beside the [Blue Lathe](/forming/lathes/) — is a bench mill with an X/Y table and a quill. We use it for two jobs, and that's on purpose: **holes that have to land exactly where the drawing says**, and **squaring the ends of tube and bar**. Everything else it could theoretically do — pockets, slots, production milling — is a mentor-led job on a machine this size.

## When to Use the Mill (and When Not To)

| You need | Machine |
| --- | --- |
| A hole in an exact location, measured from an edge | **The mill** — position with the table handwheels |
| A tube or bar end square and to length | **The mill** — face it flat |
| A quick hole where ±1/32 in is fine | [Drill press](/cutting/drill-press-shear/) — faster, more Z clearance |
| Anything round: shafts, spacers, bores | [Lathe](/forming/lathes/) |
| A pocket, slot, or flat you've not been shown | **Get a mentor.** Not a solo job |
| Big holes, hole saws, tall parts | [Drill press](/cutting/drill-press-shear/) — the mill's quill travel is short |

## Safety

Everything on this page is rotating machinery, and the lathe rules apply verbatim.

<SafetyWarning type="critical" title="Every part is clamped. No exceptions.">
The work goes in the milling vise or gets clamped to the table — never held by hand, never "just leaned against the vise jaw." A cutter that grabs an unclamped part turns it into a spinning projectile with an edge on it. If the part is too awkward to clamp, stop and ask a mentor how to fixture it.
</SafetyWarning>

<SafetyWarning type="critical" title="No gloves. Tie your hair. Nothing loose.">
**No gloves, ever**, at the mill. Long hair tied back and tucked, sleeves rolled, no drawstrings, no lanyards, no jewelry, no rings. See the [lab glove policy](/safety/gloves-and-ppe/).
</SafetyWarning>

<SafetyWarning type="critical" title="Wrenches out — same discipline as the chuck key">
The drawbar wrench and the drill-<Term id="chuck">chuck</Term> key go in your hand, do their job, and come straight back out before the spindle turns. Nothing stays parked in the spindle. Same rule as the <Term id="chuck-key">chuck key</Term> on the lathe, same consequence.
</SafetyWarning>

- **Safety goggles, not glasses.** Milling throws chips sideways and hot; glasses are open at the sides. Over-glasses goggles are in drawer 13 of the [Orange Toolbox](/storage/toolboxes/#orange-toolbox--metrology-machinist-tooling--ppe).
- Never leave the spindle running — if it's turning, you're standing at it.
- Clear chips with a brush or <Term id="chip-hook">chip hook</Term> **after the spindle stops**. Never fingers, never a rag near rotation, never compressed air (it drives grit into the ways).
- Measure with the spindle **stopped**, cutter clear of the part.
- Know where the E-stop is before you start.

## Job 1 — Drilling Located Holes

The reason to walk past the drill press and use this machine: the table moves in X and Y on <Term id="thou">thou</Term>-graduated handwheels, so a hole position is a number you dial in, not a punch mark you aim at.

1. **Clamp the part in the vise**, seated on parallels so the drill exits into clear air, not into the vise or the table. Tap it down solid — a part that rocks on the parallels is not seated.
2. **Pick a datum.** Touch off the edge of the part with the cutter or an edge finder and treat that corner as zero. Every hole gets dialed from that same zero.
3. **Always approach a coordinate from one direction.** Handwheels have backlash; if you overshoot, back well off and come in again the same way. Half the "but the dial said 2.000" holes are backlash.
4. **<Term id="spot-drill">Spot drill</Term> first**, then drill. A spot keeps the drill from walking, which is the whole point of being on this machine.
5. Set the speed for the drill diameter (see below), feed with the quill, and <Term id="peck-drilling">peck</Term> anything deeper than about two drill diameters — down, retract, clear chips, repeat. <Term id="cutting-fluid">Cutting oil</Term> in steel.
6. Ease off at breakthrough, spindle off, then unclamp and <Term id="deburr">deburr</Term> both sides.

## Job 2 — Facing Tube & Bar Ends

Chop-saw and bandsaw cuts come off a couple of degrees out of square and a little long. Facing on the mill takes them flat, square, and to a length you can actually measure from.

1. **Square the part in the vise.** The fixed jaw is your reference — clean it and the vise bed, then set the tube against it with a machinist square (Orange Toolbox drawer 2) and clamp. Chips trapped between the jaw and the part will cost you the squareness you came for.
2. **Support long tube.** Anything hanging out past the vise gets a support or a second clamp — long unsupported tube chatters and marks up.
3. **Thin walls need backing.** Box tube facing at the corners can pull the wall in. A snug plug or an offcut inside the tube stiffens it; light passes do the rest.
4. **Take small bites** — a few thou per pass with the end mill, traversing steadily by hand. If you hear chatter, back off the depth first, then the feed.
5. **Conventional, not climb.** Feed so the cutter pushes against the direction of travel. Manual machines have table backlash and climb milling snatches the work into the cutter.
6. Check with a square and calipers, spindle stopped. Deburr the inside and outside edges — a faced tube end is sharp.

<SafetyWarning type="tip" title="Face to a mark, not to a number">
For tube that's going into a welded or bolted assembly, scribe the finished length and creep up on it. It's a lot easier to take another 0.005 in off than to explain a part that's 0.020 in short.
</SafetyWarning>

## Speeds

Same formula as everywhere else in the shop — **RPM ≈ (4 × <Term id="sfm">SFM</Term>) ÷ cutter diameter in inches** — but here the diameter is the *cutter's*, not the part's. Aluminum runs roughly 200–300 SFM with HSS, mild steel 80–100.

| Cutter / drill diameter | Aluminum | Mild steel |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1/8 in | Machine's top speed | ~2500 RPM |
| 1/4 in | Machine's top speed | ~1200 RPM |
| 3/8 in | ~2400 RPM | ~800 RPM |
| 1/2 in | ~1800 RPM | ~600 RPM |
| 3/4 in and up | ~1200 RPM, low range | ~400 RPM, low range |

Small cutters fast, big cutters slow, steel slower than aluminum. Small cutters in aluminum want more RPM than this machine has, so cap at its top speed and keep the feed light rather than forcing it. Heavy cuts and larger cutters belong in the **low belt range** — that's where the torque is. Use the tach, and change belt ranges with the machine **off and unplugged**.

## Tooling & Where It Lives

Mill tooling shares the lathe's storage on the machine-shop bench:

- **End mills, drill sets, and center drills** — [Orange Toolbox drawer 5](/storage/toolboxes/#orange-toolbox--metrology-machinist-tooling--ppe) (in tubes, labeled).
- **Parallels, V-block, machinist squares, dial indicator and magnetic base** — Orange Toolbox drawer 2. This is the setup drawer; the squares and parallels are what make Job 2 come out square.
- **Drill chucks and Morse-taper arbors** — Orange Toolbox drawer 7.
- **Drawbar wrenches** stay with the machine.
- **Way oil and cutting oil** — the [Lathe T-Stak drawers](/storage/toolboxes/#lathe-t-stak-drawers) under the bench.

Put cutters back in their tubes, not loose in a drawer — an end mill that rattles against its neighbors comes back chipped. Report chipped or dull cutters instead of leaving them for the next person.

## Cleanup & Maintenance

- Spindle off, power off at the machine.
- Brush chips off the table, vise, and ways into the tray — never compressed air.
- Wipe the ways and leave a light film of way oil; the mill is on the same [maintenance schedule](/maintenance/schedules/) as the lathe.
- Cutters back in their tubes, vise handle and wrenches back on the machine, quill returned to rest.
- Aluminum offcuts to the recycling bin, steel to the scrap bin.
