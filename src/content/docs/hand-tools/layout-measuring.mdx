Every good part starts on paper — or more precisely, on a scribed line and a punched dot. Measure twice, mark once, cut once.

## Tape measures

Drawer 9's tape measures are **dual-scale — imperial fractions on one edge, metric on the other**,
and that is deliberate. Shop work is imperial; **auton paths are metric**, because that is what
WPILib and PathPlanner speak. Measuring a field distance in inches and then converting it by hand
at 11 PM is how a path ends up a foot off. Read the scale you actually need.

The magnetic tip is worth knowing about too — it hooks the end of a piece of tube so you can pull a
measurement single-handed.

## Combination squares

The combination square is three tools in one: a 90° square, a 45° miter guide, and an adjustable depth/height gauge. Use it to:

- Check that a cut end is square before you build on it.
- Mark lines parallel to an edge — lock the head at your dimension, hold a pencil or scribe at the end of the rule, and slide the head along the edge.
- Set tool heights and check depths (lock the rule, use the end as a depth probe).

Keep the locking nut snug and the rule clean. A combination square that has been dropped is suspect — check it against a known square before trusting it.

## Center punches

**Punch before every drill. No exceptions.** A drill bit walked across a part leaves a scarred surface and a hole that is not where your layout says it is. The punched dimple gives the bit's chisel point a home so it starts exactly on your mark.

1. Lay out your hole location with two crossing scribe lines.
2. Set the punch tip on the intersection, tilted so you can see the point land.
3. Stand the punch vertical and give it one firm tap with a hammer (spring-loaded automatic punches just need a push).
4. Check the dimple against your lines. If it's off, angle the punch into the correct spot and re-strike.

Then clamp the part and drill — see [Drill Press & Bench Shear](/cutting/drill-press-shear/) and [Clamps & Vises](/hand-tools/clamps-vises/).

## Calipers

Calipers are the workhorse measuring tool of the shop — good to about 0.001" when used well. **Drawer 12 of the Orange Toolbox** has the EZ Cal digital calipers, a dial caliper, a pin vise, and spare batteries. (The blue toolbox by the WAZER keeps its own labelled Calipers drawer for the fab corner — measure at the machine, put them straight back.)

### Zeroing

Before every measuring session:

1. Wipe the jaws clean — a chip or a film of oil reads as real thickness.
2. Close the jaws fully and hold them closed with light pressure.
3. Press **ZERO** (digital) or check the needle reads 0 (dial). If a dial caliper doesn't zero, rotate the bezel to zero it.
4. Open and close again to confirm it repeats at zero.

### The four measurements

- **Outside (OD)** — the big lower jaws. Square the jaws to the part and use light, consistent pressure; squeezing harder does not make the part smaller, it just gives you a wrong number.
- **Inside (ID)** — the small upper jaws. Open inside a hole or slot and rock gently to find the maximum (for holes) reading.
- **Depth** — the thin rod that extends from the end of the beam. Rest the base flat on the surface and extend the rod to the bottom of the hole.
- **Step** — the flat end faces of the beam and slider measure step heights.

<SafetyWarning type="warning" title="Calipers are instruments, not pliers">
Never use calipers as a clamp, a scribe, a pry bar, or a wrench. The jaws are hardened and ground — one abuse session and every measurement they make afterward is a lie. If you need to scribe a line, get a scribe. If you need to clamp, see [Clamps & Vises](/hand-tools/clamps-vises/).
</SafetyWarning>

## Micrometers

When a <Term id="thou">thou</Term> actually matters — bearing fits, shaft diameters you're turning on the [lathe](/forming/lathes/), press-fit pins — step up from calipers to a micrometer. **Drawer 10 of the Orange Toolbox** holds the boxed MG Tool micrometers, the iGaging EZ-View DRO Plus, and the handheld instruments.

<LabGalleryImage

- Use the **ratchet or friction thimble** to close on the part — it applies the same measuring force every time, which is the whole point. Cranking the thimble directly gives inconsistent readings and can spring the frame.
- Hold the frame, not the thimble, and let the part sit square between the anvils.
- Verify zero (or against the included standard for larger mics) before trusting a reading.
- <Term id="micrometer">Micrometers</Term> only cover a 1" range each — pick the one that matches your part.

## Precision rules & scribes

Steel rules with etched graduations beat tape measures for part layout — the markings are finer and the edge is straight enough to scribe against. Scribes scratch a fine, permanent line into metal that won't wipe off like marker. Use a scribe against a rule or square edge, tilted so the point drags into the corner of the guide edge, in one smooth pass.

## Layout fluid

For precise layout on aluminum, brush or spray on a thin coat of <Term id="layout-fluid">layout fluid</Term> (the classic blue stuff) and let it dry for a minute. A scribed line through the blue coating shows up as a bright, crisp metal line you can actually see while you work. Clean it off afterward with acetone or isopropyl alcohol — see [Surface Prep & Cleaning](/finishing/surface-prep/).

## Care rules

- **Back in the drawer, every time.** Calipers left on a bench get buried, dropped, and crushed. Orange Toolbox drawer 12 — nowhere else.
- **Don't drop them.** A dropped caliper or micrometer is no longer a measuring tool until it's been verified against a standard. If you drop one, tell a mentor — don't quietly put it back.
- Wipe tools down before they go back; chips and coolant corrode measuring faces.
- If a digital caliper reads erratically or won't zero, swap the battery — spares are in the same drawer — before reporting it broken.
