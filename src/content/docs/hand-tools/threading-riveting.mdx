## Taps & dies

Taps cut internal threads in a drilled hole; dies cut external threads on a rod. Ninety percent of what you'll tap on this team is **10-32** and **1/4-20** in aluminum.

### Tap drill sizes

The hole you drill before tapping must be the **tap drill** size — too small and the tap seizes and snaps, too big and the threads are shallow and weak. Laminated tap/drill charts live in the top drawer of the red toolbox — check the chart, don't guess. The two you'll use constantly: **#21 drill for 10-32** and **#7 drill for 1/4-20**.

### Hand tapping, step by step

1. Drill the tap-drill-size hole and deburr it (a slight chamfer helps the tap start).
2. Mount the tap in a tap handle — never pliers or a drill.
3. Add **cutting fluid**. Aluminum taps dry are how taps break; a drop or two makes the whole job smoother.
4. Start the tap **dead straight**. Check it against a square in two directions after the first couple of turns — a crooked start can't be corrected later, only redrilled.
5. Advance about half a turn, then **back off a quarter turn to break the chip**. Feel the little snap as the chip shears. Repeat: half a turn forward, quarter back, all the way through.
6. If it suddenly gets hard to turn, **stop**. Back out, clear chips, re-lubricate. Forcing it is how taps snap.
7. Back the tap all the way out, blow the chips clear, and test with a screw.

<SafetyWarning type="warning" title="Broken taps are a big deal">
Taps are hardened and brittle — snap one off in a hole and it is harder than any drill bit you can throw at it. The part is usually scrap. The fixes are all prevention: right tap drill, cutting fluid, straight start, and backing off to break chips. If a tap breaks anyway, take it to a mentor — do not attack it with a drill.
</SafetyWarning>

Dies work the same way in reverse: chamfer the rod end, start the die square in its stock, cut with the same forward-and-back rhythm and plenty of fluid.

### The servo tapping arm

For batch tapping, the shop has a folding-arm electric tapping machine mounted on the workbench near the Evolution chop saw. It holds the tap perfectly vertical, drives it at controlled speed with a torque clutch, and reverses out — no crooked starts, and far fewer broken taps than hand tapping. Get trained on it by a mentor before first use, and still use cutting fluid.

## Hand rivet guns

Blind (pop) rivets join sheet and tube fast, light, and permanently — no access to the back side needed. The manual rivet guns live in the blue toolbox riveting drawer; for high-volume riveting use the [M12 Riveting Tools](/portable-power/m12-riveting/).

1. Drill the hole to match the rivet diameter (1/8" rivet, 1/8" hole — a #30 drill gives a good fit).
2. Match the rivet's **grip range** to the total stack thickness you're joining.
3. Insert the rivet mandrel into the gun's nosepiece, rivet body through the work.
4. Hold the parts clamped tight together and squeeze until the mandrel snaps off.
5. Spent mandrels go in the trash, not on the floor — they're sharp and they find tires and feet.

## Rivnut tools

Rivnuts (rivet nuts) give you real machine threads in thin wall tube or sheet where a tapped hole would strip — the go-to for bolting onto box tubing. The rivetnuts drawer has the red-handled manual installation tools and bags of aluminum and brass rivnuts sorted by size.

### Setting a rivnut

1. Drill the hole to the size the rivnut calls for (check the packaging or the tap/drill chart area — it's larger than the thread size).
2. Thread the rivnut fully onto the tool's mandrel, flange against the nosepiece.
3. Insert into the hole, hold the tool square to the surface, and squeeze the handles. The body collapses and mushrooms against the back side.
4. Compress until snug — then **stop**. Over-compressing strips the rivnut's internal threads or spins it loose in the hole.
5. Unthread the mandrel and test with a bolt.

A rivnut that spins in its hole is a miserable thing to remove — square hole edges, the right hole size, and firm (not maximal) compression prevent it. For repetitive installs there are also drill-attachment rivnut mandrels that spin the rivnut set with a drill — use a clutch-limited drill on low, because an impact driver will destroy the threads instantly.

**Aluminum vs. brass:** aluminum rivnuts are the lightweight default for robot structures; brass ones are stronger in the threads and less prone to spinning out — use them where a bolt gets removed often.
