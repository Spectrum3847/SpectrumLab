Two hands are for the tool, not the part. Almost every shop injury story starts with "I was just holding it." Clamps and vises hold the work so the tool can't spin it, launch it, or drag your hand into the cut.

<SafetyWarning type="critical" title="Clamp the work for EVERY drill press operation">
Never hand-hold a part on the drill press — not for one hole, not for a second. When a bit grabs (and it will, especially breaking through aluminum or in polycarbonate), an unclamped part becomes a spinning blade at hand height. Use a drill-press vise or clamp the part to the table, every single time. See [Drill Press & Bench Shear](/cutting/drill-press-shear/).
</SafetyWarning>

## Bench vises

The Yost bench vise on the workbench is the shop's general-purpose third hand — filing, deburring, hacksawing, pressing small parts together, holding tube while you drill rivnut holes.

<LabGalleryImage
  src="bench-vise-klutch-shear.jpg"
  alt="Yost bench vise and Klutch benchtop shear on the workbench"
  caption="The Yost 465 bench vise on the main workbench, next to the Klutch shear."
/>

- **Use soft jaws on finished parts.** The vise's serrated steel jaws exist to bite — and they'll bite your machined, powder-coated, or 3D-printed part just as happily. Slip aluminum, copper, or plastic jaw covers over the steel jaws for anything you care about the surface of.
- Tighten by hand on the handle. No hammers on the handle, no pipe extensions — if hand-tight isn't enough, you're using the wrong workholding.
- Clamp near the middle of the jaws, and support long stock so it isn't cantilevered.
- Don't hammer sideways on work in the vise or use the vise as an anvil — the light hammering flat is behind the jaws, and serious pressing belongs on the [arbor press](/forming/arbor-press/).

## Drill-press vises

The small drill-press vise lives in **drawer 11 of the [Orange Toolbox](/storage/toolboxes/#orange-toolbox--metrology-machinist-tooling--ppe)**; the bigger ones sit out on the bench by the Klutch shear. Drill-press vises have flat, grooved jaws and slots for bolting to the drill press table. Park the part in the vise, snug it, line up your center-punched mark under the bit, and hold or bolt the vise itself. Even unbolted, a vise gives the work enough mass and a handle for you to resist grab — but bolt it down for larger bits, which grab hardest.

## Kant-Twist clamps

Kant-Twist clamps close with a parallel, cantilever action — the pads come together flat instead of arcing like a C-clamp screw, so they don't twist or walk the parts out of alignment as you tighten. That makes them the first choice for holding two machined parts in alignment while you drill or pin them, and for clamping work to machine tables where a shifting clamp ruins the setup. Copper-coated pads also won't chew the surface the way a bare C-clamp screw does.

## C-clamps

The classic. Huge clamping force per dollar, in every size. The screw tip rotates as it tightens, so it can scar the surface and drag parts slightly — put a scrap pad under the screw side on anything finished, and lead with Kant-Twists when alignment matters. C-clamps and Kant-Twists live with the fab-corner gear in the [blue toolbox](/storage/toolboxes/#blue-toolbox--fabrication-support) — in one of its unlabelled drawers, which is exactly the reason that box needs labelling.

## 90° corner clamps

Corner clamps hold two pieces at a right angle so you can drill, rivet, or weld the joint square. Check the joint with a combination square anyway — a corner clamp is only as square as the faces you gave it. They're the fast way to keep frame corners honest while gusseting box tube.

## Bar clamps

Bar clamps (and their quick-grip trigger cousins) span the big stuff: gluing, holding bumper frames together, pulling assemblies into place, and clamping sheets to the bench. The shop's bar clamps hang across the top of the rolling material cart — take them back there, because a bar clamp on the loose is the hardest tool in the shop to find.

## Snap ring pliers

Not a clamp, but the same "hold the thing so it can't fight you" family, and they live in red
toolbox **drawer 4** with the pliers. The shop standardised on **KNIPEX** snap ring pliers after
trying cheaper ones: the tips don't splay under load, they actually hold a ring instead of
flinging it, and they open wide enough to work around a hex shaft.

- A snap ring that pings across the shop is gone. Cup your free hand over it, or work inside a
  box.
- Don't over-spread a ring — stretched rings don't seat in their groove and walk out under load.
- Safety goggles. This is a spring-steel projectile with a grudge.

## Quick rules

- Clamp it before you cut it, drill it, file it, or sand it.
- Pad the jaws on any surface you'd be sad to scratch.
- Two clamps beat one — a single clamp is a pivot.
- Take clamps off and put them back when the glue is dry or the hole is drilled. Clamps left on parts are clamps nobody can find.
