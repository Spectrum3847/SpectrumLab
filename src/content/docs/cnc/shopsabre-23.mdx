The ShopSabre 23 is our CNC router: a 30 × 40 in bed with a 3.5 HP variable-speed router spindle on ball screws and linear rails. It cuts aluminum plate, plywood, and plastics with pockets, countersinks, and 2.5D features the waterjet can't do. It is a rotating-tool machine — the strictest no-gloves machine in the shop.

## Safety

<SafetyWarning type="critical" title="NO GLOVES. Ever.">
The spindle turns a carbide cutter at up to full router RPM. A glove that touches a spinning tool does not tear away — it wraps and pulls your hand in. Remove gloves before you touch this machine, along with rings, watches, and loose sleeves, and tie back long hair. Full policy: [PPE & the glove policy](/safety/gloves-and-ppe/).
</SafetyWarning>

<SafetyWarning type="warning" title="Know where the E-stop is before you press start">
Point at the E-stop before your first cut of the day. If the machine does anything you did not program — plunge into a clamp, chatter violently, pull the part loose — hit it. E-stops are free; endmills and fingers are not.
</SafetyWarning>

- Safety glasses always; hearing protection while cutting. Aluminum chips come off hot and fast.
- Never reach into the work envelope while the spindle is turning — not to grab a freed part, not to clear a chip.
- Clear chips with a vacuum or brush **with the spindle stopped**, never with your hands, and never with compressed air aimed toward people.
- Broken endmill? Stop, E-stop if needed, and clear the machine with the spindle fully stopped before investigating.

## Software & Workflow (CAM)

1. **Model in Onshape or Fusion.** Design with the tooling in mind: inside corners get a radius at least as big as the endmill radius, and pockets deeper than about 2× tool diameter need a rethink.
2. **CAM the part** (Onshape CAM Studio, Fusion Manufacture, or VCarve Pro for simple wood jobs — the machine ships with VCarve). Define stock, set the WCS origin at a repeatable spot (typically top of stock, front-left corner), and build toolpaths: adaptive/pocket for clearing, 2D contour for profiles, with **tabs on every through-cut part**.
3. **Simulate.** Watch the whole toolpath. Look for plunges outside the stock, rapids through clamps, and tabs actually present.
4. **Post-process** with the ShopSabre post to generate G-code.
5. **Transfer to the controller PC** at the machine and load the job in the machine control software.
6. **Set your work origin on the machine** to match the CAM origin exactly — same corner, same top-of-stock. Origin mismatch is the classic first-job crash.

## Feeds & Speeds — Starting Points

**Starting points only.** Every one of these needs adjusting by ear and by chip: good aluminum cutting sounds like a steady tearing hiss and makes distinct chips, not dust or squeal. Dust means feed too slow (rubbing); squeal and chatter mean too fast or too deep.

| Material | Tool | RPM | Feed | Pass Depth |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 6061 aluminum | 1/4 in single-flute carbide | 18,000 | 45 IPM | 0.030 in |
| 6061 aluminum (finish pass) | 1/4 in single-flute carbide | 18,000 | 30 IPM | 0.010 in side |
| Plywood / MDF | 1/4 in two-flute downcut | 18,000 | 120 IPM | 0.25 in |
| Polycarbonate | 1/4 in single-flute (O-flute) | 16,000 | 90 IPM | 0.10 in |
| HDPE / Delrin | 1/4 in single-flute | 16,000 | 100 IPM | 0.15 in |

### Why single-flute for aluminum

Aluminum welds itself to hot cutters. A single-flute carbide endmill gives each cutting edge a huge chip pocket, so chips evacuate before they re-melt onto the tool. Two- and three-flute mills pack chips, weld up, and snap. **Single-flute carbide is the default for all aluminum on this machine** — and keep chips cleared so the tool never re-cuts them.

## Workholding

The part coming loose mid-cut is the most common router failure. Plan holding before you post:

- **Screws to the spoilboard** are the standard: run drywall or wood screws through the waste margin of your stock into the spoilboard. Put screw locations in your CAM drawing so no toolpath crosses one — a screw strike destroys the endmill and throws shrapnel.
- **Tabs** on every through-cut part. Without tabs, the last millimeter of a profile cut frees the part into the spinning cutter. Size tabs so they hold in aluminum (wider/thicker than wood defaults) and cut them free afterward with a saw or flush cutter, then file smooth.
- Clamps only at the stock edges, physically below the toolpath plane or fully outside the machining area, and never in a rapid path.
- Thin aluminum sheet likes lots of screws — it wants to lift and chatter between fixings.

## Operating Instructions

1. Power on the machine and controller PC; home the machine.
2. Install the endmill: clean collet, insert with adequate grip length, torque the nut with both wrenches.
3. Fix your stock to the spoilboard (screws + planned waste margin) and verify flatness.
4. Set XY origin at your CAM origin corner, then set Z zero to top of stock (or spoilboard, matching your CAM setup) using the touch-off procedure.
5. Load the G-code and sanity-check the preview on the controller.
6. Set spindle RPM per the table, start dust collection or have the vacuum staged.
7. Start the job **with feed override reduced**. Listen to the first entry — if it sounds right, roll override up to 100%.
8. Stay at the controller for the whole job, hand near the feed hold/E-stop. Vacuum chips between passes or during tool changes **only with the spindle stopped** — chip re-cutting is what kills single-flute endmills in aluminum.
9. When the job ends, wait for the spindle to fully stop, jog clear, then remove screws and lift the stock.
10. Cut parts free of their tabs at the bench, deburr, and vacuum the machine.

## Cleanup & Maintenance

After every job:

- Vacuum the bed, T-slots/spoilboard surface, and rail covers. Aluminum chips left on rails grind into the motion system.
- Pull every screw out of the spoilboard and return endmills and wrenches to their drawers.
- Wipe the collet and spindle nose; a chip inside the collet ruins the next person's runout.
- Power down the controller and machine.

### Spoilboard maintenance

- The spoilboard is sacrificial — light scoring is normal, but craters and deep grooves let parts flex and cut inaccurately.
- When the surface is chewed up or no longer flat, it gets **fly-cut (surfaced)** with the surfacing bit — a mentor-led job on the [maintenance schedule](/maintenance/schedules/).
- Help it last: set through-cut depths just a few thou past the stock bottom, not deep trenches into the board.

Flat aluminum profiles with no pockets are often faster on the [WAZER Pro waterjet](/waterjet/wazer-pro/); thin sheet metal cuts fastest on the [xTool MetalFab fiber laser](/lasers/xtool-metalfab/); wood and acrylic without 2.5D features belong on the [Thunder Nova 35](/lasers/thunder-nova-35/).
