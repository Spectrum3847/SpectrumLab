The tapping arm is how we thread holes in batches. It is a folding articulated arm on the
workbench near the [Evolution chop saws](/cutting/evolution-saws/): you pull the head over the
hole, squeeze the trigger, and it drives the tap in dead straight at a controlled speed, then
reverses out. A torque clutch slips before the tap does.

It exists because hand tapping a plate with twenty 10-32 holes is slow, and every one of those
holes is a chance to start crooked or snap a tap. This machine removes both problems, which is
why the team designs a lot of tapped standoffs and threaded plates into the robot in the first
place.

<SafetyWarning type="warning" title="No gloves on the tapping arm">
The tap and <Term id="collet">collet</Term> spin. A glove that touches a rotating tap pulls your hand in with it — this is
the same rule as the [lathe](/forming/lathes/) and it is not negotiable. Bare hands, sleeves
rolled, long hair tied back. See [PPE & the glove policy](/safety/gloves-and-ppe/).
</SafetyWarning>

Everything we tap regularly sits comfortably inside the M3–M16 range: **10-32** is about M5 and
**1/4-20** is about M6. You will not run out of capacity on a robot part.

<SafetyWarning type="tip" title="Two spec numbers on the listing disagree">
The product listing advertises **0–375 rpm** while its own spec table says **312 rpm**, and the
title says **M3–M16** while a bullet claims M3–M36. The 16 mm maximum collet capacity is the
number that settles it — treat this as an **M3–M16** machine. Speed is variable anyway and you
should be running well below the top of the range in aluminum. Check the machine's own plate
before trusting any figure here.
</SafetyWarning>

## Why we have this one

We used to tap with a **Vevor pneumatic tapping station** (M3–M12, vertical, quick-change),
bought in the fall of 2023 and documented in
[Lab Upgrades](https://www.spectrum3847.org/seasons/2024/#2024-01-05-lab-upgrades). It kept a
#10-32 spiral point tap loaded at all times and made tapping shafts fast enough that the 2024
robot was designed around tapped standoffs.

In late 2025 we replaced it with this electric machine. Per the
[2026 season kickoff post](https://www.spectrum3847.org/seasons/2026/#2026-01-07-welcome-to-our-15th-build-blog):
it is easier to use, it can tap automatically rather than needing the operator to drive each
stroke, and **the clutch was adjusted specifically to reduce tap breakage**.

It is also one of the machines new members learn first — the
[Day 40 post](https://www.spectrum3847.org/seasons/2026/#2026-02-19-day-40) lists the tapping arm
alongside the Thunder Laser and xTool MetalFab as tools that are "very easy" to train on.

## The drill-and-tap station

The arm is only half of it. The bench around it was built as a combined drill and tap station:

- A **spoil board** area you can drill straight through without hitting the bench.
- **T-track** for clamping plates flat and fast.
- **Vises clamped to the table** for holding shafts and small parts upright.

Drill the hole, slide the part over, tap it — without re-fixturing. Keep the station clear;
it stops being fast the moment someone stacks parts on it.

## Tooling & consumables

- **Collets** — the 8-piece set covers the tap shank sizes we use. One tap per collet size; do
  not force an undersized shank into a bigger collet.
- **SAE vs metric is the real friction.** The machine is specified in metric (M3–M16) but we tap
  **SAE** threads — 10-32 and 1/4-20. Finding tap holders that fit SAE shanks is the recurring
  annoyance with every arm in this class. The community answer is a **torque-control collet**
  sized for the thread (teams pair these arms with a **Lyndex T05-010** for 10-32); it slips at a
  set torque and is what actually stops taps snapping. If a collet is missing or damaged, tell a
  mentor — do not substitute a loose fit.
- **Spiral point taps** — the machine's default tap is **#10-32 spiral point**, kept installed
  so the most common job needs zero setup. Spiral point ("gun") taps push chips forward out of
  the hole, which is what you want in a <Term id="through-hole">through hole</Term>.
- **Cutting fluid** — still required. Powered tapping does not make dry aluminum safe to tap;
  it just makes the mistake faster.
- Spare taps live in **drawer 7 (Thread Taps)** of the red toolbox — see
  [Threading & Riveting](/hand-tools/threading-riveting/).

## How the automatic cycle works

This is the part that makes the electric machine worth the swap, and it is worth understanding
before you use it.

- **Depth is counted in rotations, not distance.** You set the number of turns; combined with the
  tap's thread pitch, that is your thread depth.
- **Torque decides when counting starts** — the machine knows the tap has engaged the hole when it
  sees load, and begins counting from there.
- **It auto-retracts at the bottom.** Start the tap in the hole, let go, and the machine taps to
  depth and backs itself out unattended.
- **Auto-reverse breaks chips.** You can set it to back up after a number of rotations and then go
  forward again — a peck cycle. This is the single biggest reason it needs less training than the
  old pneumatic arm: chip packing was what broke taps.

## Operating steps

1. **Drill first.** The tapped hole is only as good as the <Term id="tap-drill">tap drill</Term> under it. Wrong drill size
   is the single most common cause of a snapped tap.
2. **Set the tap in the collet** and confirm it is seated and running true.
3. **Set depth (rotations) and the auto-reverse interval** for the tap and hole you have — not
   whatever the last person left dialed in. <Term id="blind-hole">Blind holes</Term> need conservative depth.
4. **Set the clutch** for the tap size. Small taps need less torque; the clutch is what protects
   the tap.
5. **Add cutting fluid.**
6. **Bring the head over the hole** and let the arm find vertical. Do not fight the arm sideways
   to reach — reposition the part instead. A side load is a crooked thread.
7. **Start the tap and let go.** The cycle runs itself and retracts. Guiding gently is fine;
   forcing or holding it down is not.
8. **Blow the chips clear** and test with an actual screw before you tap the next forty holes.
   One test hole catches a wrong tap; forty do not.

<SafetyWarning type="critical" title="If the clutch slips, stop">
A slipping clutch means the tap is binding — chips packed in the flutes, no lubricant, a
too-small hole, or the tap bottoming out in a blind hole. Every one of those gets worse if you
keep going. Back out, clear the chips, re-lubricate, and check the hole size. Do not turn the
clutch up to force it through: the clutch is the only thing standing between you and a broken
tap in a finished part.
</SafetyWarning>

## Common failures

- **Snapped tap** — almost always the tap drill was undersized, or the tap ran dry. Take it to a
  mentor; a broken tap is harder than any drill bit and the part is usually scrap.
- **Crooked or cross-threaded hole** — the arm was pulled sideways to reach the hole. Move the
  part, not the arm.
- **Threads look torn or oversized** — dull tap, or the speed is too high for the material. Slow
  down and swap the tap.
- **Clutch slipping immediately** — clutch set too low for the tap size, or the hole is wrong.
  Verify the hole before touching the clutch.
- **Tap binds in a blind hole** — spiral point taps push chips ahead of them, which packs the
  bottom of a blind hole. Use a spiral flute tap for blind holes, or clear and re-enter.

## Cleanup & Maintenance

- **Blow or brush the chips off** the machine and the station when you finish. Aluminum chips
  packed around the collet are how the next person gets a crooked tap.
- Wipe cutting fluid off the bench — it spreads onto parts headed for
  [powder coating](/finishing/powder-coating/), where oil ruins adhesion.
- Return the tap to its drawer unless it is the standing **#10-32**, which stays installed.
- Report a dull tap, a slipping-under-no-load clutch, or any arm play to a mentor rather than
  working around it.
