Ninety percent of print quality and part strength is decided before you touch a single slicer
setting: it comes from **which way the part sits on the plate**. Get orientation right, then
supports, then walls and <Term id="infill">infill</Term>. Material choice lives on the
[Filament Guide](/3d-printing/filament-guide/).

## Orientation for strength

A 3D print is a stack of welded layers. It is strong along the layers and weak between them —
a printed part pulls apart between layers like string cheese, at a fraction of the strength it
has in the other directions.

**Rule: layer lines must run perpendicular to the load.** Never let a load try to peel layers
apart.

- A bracket that gets pushed sideways? Print it flat so the force runs along the layers.
- A hook or lever? Print it lying on its side, so the curve is drawn within each layer — not
  standing up, where the bend puts tension straight across layer bonds.
- A part loaded in several directions? Orient for the biggest load, add fillets at inside
  corners (cracks start at sharp corners), or split the part into two prints, each oriented for
  its own load, and bolt them together.

Two more orientation wins:

- **Holes print roundest pointing up** (axis vertical). Horizontal holes come out slightly
  D-shaped — design them 0.2 mm oversize or drill/ream them after.
- **The plate side is the flattest, best-looking face.** Put mating or cosmetic surfaces down
  when you can.

## The slicer priority hierarchy

When slicing a functional robot part, tune in **exactly this order**. The list is sorted by how
much each setting actually buys you in strength per gram — students always reach for the infill
slider, which is fifth.

1. **<Term id="perimeter">Perimeter</Term> loops (walls).** The single biggest lever on bending and torsional strength — it
   is the I-beam principle, and the walls are the flanges. Run **6–8 walls** on a 0.4 mm nozzle
   or **4–5 walls** on a 0.6 mm nozzle.
2. **Extrusion temperature and low fan.** Print at the **high end** of the manufacturer's
   temperature range with part cooling at **20–40%**. Hotter plastic and slower cooling means
   more polymer chain entanglement across the layer boundary — this is free interlayer strength.
3. **Top / bottom solid layers.** **5–7 solid layers**, minimum 1.2–1.6 mm total thickness.
   This is what stops bolt crush and fastener pull-through; thin top skins tear out around
   washers.
4. **Line width.** Set to **115–125% of nozzle diameter** — 0.50–0.52 mm on a 0.4 mm nozzle,
   0.70–0.75 mm on a 0.6 mm. Wider beads mean more thermal contact area between layers.
5. **Infill percentage.** **25–40%** for structural brackets. Reserve 100% / all-walls for
   dead-axle spacers and clamp blocks where the part is a solid chunk by design — see the
   **Maximum Strength** preset below.
6. **Infill pattern.** **Gyroid** for isotropic 3D strength — impacts arrive from directions
   nobody predicted, and gyroid is the pattern with no preferred one. Use **adaptive cubic** on
   big low-detail parts, where subdividing away from the surfaces buys time you actually feel.
   Avoid grid and triangles — the nozzle drags back across its own crossings, which costs
   quality and speed for no strength gain.
7. **<Term id="layer-height">Layer height</Term>.** **0.20 mm** on a 0.4 mm nozzle, **0.28–0.32 mm** on a 0.6 mm.

<SafetyWarning type="tip" title="Reality-check the part, not the slicer">
If a printed part breaks at practice, look at the break. Clean separation along a layer line
means orientation was wrong — no amount of infill would have saved it. Reprint rotated, not
denser.
</SafetyWarning>

Two more joint rules that outrank everything on the list above: add washers or design bosses so
bolt heads never bear on thin printed faces, and use **heat-set inserts** instead of threading
plastic for anything serviced more than once.

## Support strategy

The best support is no support. Before enabling anything, try to design or orient the need
away:

1. **Rotate the part.** A shelf-shaped <Term id="overhang">overhang</Term> often becomes a supported wall from another
   angle.
2. **Chamfer instead of overhang.** A 45° chamfer under a boss or ledge prints clean with zero
   support.
3. **Split the part** at a flat face and glue/bolt the halves — two easy prints beat one print
   full of scarred support surfaces.
4. Remember anything **under 45° from vertical prints fine unsupported** on our machines.

When you genuinely need supports:

- **Tree (organic) supports — the default.** They use less filament, pull off in one satisfying
  piece, and barely scar the part. Best for anything with scattered small overhangs or organic
  shapes.
- **Normal (grid) supports** — use for large flat ceilings (a big horizontal underside), where
  trees get flimsy and a uniform interface leaves a flatter surface.
- Single-material supports want a **top Z distance of about 0.2 mm** (one layer). If supports
  weld on, increase the gap; if the surface above sags, decrease it.
- Supported surfaces are always the ugliest surface on the part. Face them where nobody looks
  and no part mates.

## Dual-nozzle support strategies

The H2D and X2D have a second nozzle, which lets a support tower print in a different material
from the part. The house profiles default to **same-material supports with a 1-layer Z gap** — the
support prints in the part's own material and breaks away at the gap. That is the fast choice
and it needs no setup. When the finish on a supported face actually matters, the zero-gap PLA
<Term id="support-interface">interface</Term> below is the option.

<SafetyWarning type="tip" title="Turn off the prime tower for true dual-nozzle prints">
Whenever a print assigns each nozzle its own dedicated material — a PLA support interface,
PPA-GF supports on a second hotend, anything where **the AMS is not the thing switching
filament** — turn off **Print Settings → Others → Prime tower** before slicing. Bambu Studio
ships `enable_prime_tower` **on by default**, including in the dual-nozzle process presets, so
this does not happen automatically; you have to uncheck it yourself, every time.

The prime tower exists to purge and rebuild pressure when a single nozzle swaps between
filaments fed through the AMS. On a real dual-nozzle job, material A stays on nozzle 1 and
material B stays on nozzle 2 for the entire print — nothing is ever pushed back through the same
hotend, so there is nothing to purge. Leaving it on just burns plate space and print time for no
quality benefit. If a job ever *does* route more than one filament through the same physical
nozzle (AMS-fed color or material swaps), leave the prime tower on — that case still needs it.

Measured on an X2D zero-gap test slice (same model, same 108 nozzle changes): with the tower on,
the plate came out to 23.62 g and 1h50m, **54% of that weight (12.69 g) was the prime tower
alone** — more than the part itself. Tower off: 10.93 g and 1h9m, same part, same supports. Half
the filament, 41 fewer minutes, nothing else changed.
</SafetyWarning>

### Zero-gap PLA interface

Not the default, and it is worth knowing why. A PLA interface gets you an underside that looks
injection-molded, but it costs a nozzle change on every layer that carries interface, and
somebody has to load PLA in slot 2 before the print starts. Same-material supports need neither.
Reach for this when the finish on a supported face matters — not by habit.

**H2D and X2D only** — the X1Cs have a single nozzle. Slicer profile:
**`06 Robot Part - PLA Support`**. Use for **72D TPU and PETG-GF**.

- **Main part and support towers:** the same functional material as the part (PETG-GF or
  72D TPU).
- **Support interface layer:** standard **white PLA** in Nozzle 2.
- **Top / bottom Z distance:** **0.0 mm — zero gap.**
- **Interface layers:** 2–3.

*Why it works:* PLA will not chemically weld to polyurethanes or copolyesters. The interface
touches the part with no gap at all, so the underside comes out looking injection-molded — and
then the whole support snaps off clean, because the bond was never there to begin with.

<SafetyWarning type="tip" title="Set the interface, not just the gap">
Zero gap without a PLA interface layer welds the support to the part permanently. If you set
Z distance to 0.0 mm, you must also assign the interface extruder and enable 2–3 interface
layers, or you are printing a solid block.
</SafetyWarning>

### PPA-GF supports

PLA cannot be used here — PPA-GF prints at 310 °C, which cooks PLA into a plug.

- **Standard geometry:** single-material **PPA-GF tree supports** with a **0.22 mm top Z gap**.
  The glass fibers create a clean break plane on their own.
- **Geometry you cannot reach with pliers:** we do not stock a high-temperature or soluble
  support material, so there is no fallback. Reorient the part, split it, or design the overhang
  out — do not start a PPA-GF print you have no way to clean up.

## Overhang & bridging limits

- **45° is the safe overhang limit** for every material we stock. PLA with full fan can push
  toward 60°; PETG-GF and the nylons start drooping past 45–50°.
- **Bridges** (flat spans anchored at both ends) print fine to roughly **20–30 mm in PLA** with
  100% fan, less in PETG-GF, and less again in low-fan materials like PPA-GF and PC.
  Longer than that: add a support pillar mid-span or chamfer the opening.
- Design tip: turn rectangular horizontal openings into **teardrops or 45° diamonds** and they
  print with no support at all.

## House presets

Each card names the **Spectrum3847 filament and process presets** to pick in Bambu Studio — the
numbered entries in the dropdowns, published from the
[3DPrinterSettings](https://github.com/Spectrum3847/3DPrinterSettings) repo. Select those two
first; the values below are what they set, so you can check the profile did what you expect and
adjust from there.

Start from these, then adjust. **The values below are for a 0.4 mm nozzle, which means the H2D and
the X2D.** Most of the lab's machines are not 0.4 mm: the **X1Cs run 0.6 mm** and so do all twelve
**Prusa Minis**. On any 0.6 mm machine, convert using the
[priority hierarchy](#the-slicer-priority-hierarchy) — **4–5 walls, 0.70–0.75 mm line width, and
0.28–0.32 mm layers** — instead of typing these numbers in as-is. Copying a 0.4 mm profile onto a
0.6 mm machine under-extrudes badly, and it is the most common way a "known good" preset produces
a weak part.

Worth reading next to [Orientation for strength](#orientation-for-strength) — it lands on the same
rule from a different direction: design so the largest forces run across the XY plane, and split
the part when no single orientation serves every face. Its "printed unit test" is our
prototype-in-PLA fit check under another name.

Two things it covers that this page does not. **Fillet the edges normal to the plate and chamfer
the ones sitting on it** — fillets cut stress concentrations and warping, chamfers make the part
easier to release and stop the first layer splaying, and a chamfer on a hole helps a fit line up.
And **reach for hardware before printing a feature**: heat-set inserts rather than tapped plastic
(which this page already requires), plus dowel pins for wear surfaces and alignment, and bushings
where you need a concentric running fit.

Skip the continuous-fiber passages. Markforged machines lay continuous strand along a toolpath;
ours chop fiber into the filament, which is why our glass fill buys stiffness rather than
routed reinforcement. Everything else on the page applies to FFF unchanged.
