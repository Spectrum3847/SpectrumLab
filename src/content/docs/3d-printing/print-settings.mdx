Ninety percent of print quality and part strength is decided before you touch a single slicer
setting: it comes from **which way the part sits on the plate**. Get orientation right, then
supports, then walls and infill. Material choice lives on the
[Filament Guide](/3d-printing/filament-guide/).

## Orientation for strength

A 3D print is a stack of welded layers. It is strong along the layers and weak between them —
a printed part pulls apart between layers like string cheese, at a fraction of the strength it
has in the other directions.

**Rule: layer lines must run perpendicular to the load.** Never let a load try to peel layers
apart.

- A bracket that gets pushed sideways? Print it flat so the force runs along the layers.
- A hook or lever? Print it lying on its side, so the curve is drawn within each layer — not
  standing up, where the bend puts tension straight across layer bonds.
- A part loaded in several directions? Orient for the biggest load, add fillets at inside
  corners (cracks start at sharp corners), or split the part into two prints, each oriented for
  its own load, and bolt them together.

Two more orientation wins:

- **Holes print roundest pointing up** (axis vertical). Horizontal holes come out slightly
  D-shaped — design them 0.2 mm oversize or drill/ream them after.
- **The plate side is the flattest, best-looking face.** Put mating or cosmetic surfaces down
  when you can.

## Support strategy

The best support is no support. Before enabling anything, try to design or orient the need
away:

1. **Rotate the part.** A shelf-shaped overhang often becomes a supported wall from another
   angle.
2. **Chamfer instead of overhang.** A 45° chamfer under a boss or ledge prints clean with zero
   support.
3. **Split the part** at a flat face and glue/bolt the halves — two easy prints beat one print
   full of scarred support surfaces.
4. Remember anything **under 45° from vertical prints fine unsupported** on our machines.

When you genuinely need supports:

- **Tree (organic) supports — the default.** They use less filament, pull off in one satisfying
  piece, and barely scar the part. Best for anything with scattered small overhangs or organic
  shapes.
- **Normal (grid) supports** — use for large flat ceilings (a big horizontal underside), where
  trees get flimsy and a uniform interface leaves a flatter surface.
- Set a **support/part top Z distance of about 0.2 mm** (one layer) and enable a support
  interface for surfaces that matter. If supports weld on, increase the gap; if the surface
  above sags, decrease it.
- Supported surfaces are always the ugliest surface on the part. Face them where nobody looks
  and no part mates.

## Overhang & bridging limits

- **45° is the safe overhang limit** for every material we stock. PLA with full fan can push
  toward 60°; PETG and PA-CF start drooping past 45–50°.
- **Bridges** (flat spans anchored at both ends) print fine to roughly **20–30 mm in PLA** with
  100% fan, less in PETG (it sags) and less again in low-fan materials like PA-CF and PC.
  Longer than that: add a support pillar mid-span or chamfer the opening.
- Design tip: turn rectangular horizontal openings into **teardrops or 45° diamonds** and they
  print with no support at all.

## Walls & infill for FRC parts

Students always reach for the infill slider. Wrong slider — **walls carry the load.** Going
from 2 to 4 walls adds far more strength than going from 15% to 50% infill, at less weight.

- **Structural parts:** 4–6 walls, 5 top/bottom layers, 30–50% gyroid infill.
- **Everything else:** 2–3 walls, 10–15% infill.
- **Gyroid** is the house infill — strong in every direction, prints fast, and handles curved
  interior geometry well. Grid is fine for flat quick stuff.
- **100% infill is almost never the answer.** Past ~60% you add weight faster than strength.
  If a part needs 100% infill to survive, it wants to be PA-CF, thicker, or aluminum.
- Bolted joints: add washers or design bosses so bolt heads never bear on thin printed faces,
  and use heat-set inserts instead of threading plastic for anything serviced more than once.

<SafetyWarning type="tip" title="Reality-check the part, not the slicer">
If a printed part breaks at practice, look at the break. Clean separation along a layer line
means orientation was wrong — no amount of infill would have saved it. Reprint rotated, not
denser.
</SafetyWarning>
