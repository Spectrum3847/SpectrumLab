When a part will not fit the Bambus' 256 mm or the H2D's 350 mm envelope, the two big Crealitys
take over: the **CR-6 Max** (400 mm cube) and the **CR-10 Max** (450 × 450 × 470 mm). They print
full-size intake prototypes, field-element mockups, TRI props, and other big single pieces in
one go — no seams, no glue joints.

Set expectations honestly: these are **older, open-frame bedslingers**. They are slower than
the Bambus, fussier, and a big print is measured in days, not hours. They earn their bench
space by doing the one thing nothing else in the lab can: printing *big*.

## Before you go big: do you actually need to?

- **Split it first.** Two 250 mm halves on a [Bambu](/3d-printing/bambu-fleet/) with a bolted
  or pinned joint usually print faster, stronger, and more reliably than one 500 mm gamble.
- Go large-format when the part genuinely must be monolithic: continuous curves, sealed
  volumes, or geometry where a joint would be the weak point.
- Budget real calendar time. A plate-filling print can run 30+ hours — schedule it so the
  critical hours land while the lab is open.

The CR-6 Max levels by pressing the nozzle itself against the bed with a strain gauge — no
Z-offset knob and no probe offset to manage, but it means **a clean nozzle tip is part of the
leveling system**. A blob of plastic on the nozzle becomes a leveling error across the whole
plate.

## Safety

<SafetyWarning type="warning" title="Open frame, long reach">
These machines have no enclosure and a bed that slings almost half a meter of hot glass back
and forth. Keep the swing zone clear — bags, spools, and elbows included — and never reach
over a moving bed.
</SafetyWarning>

- Bed runs up to 100 °C and is a large thermal mass: it stays burn-hot long after the print
  ends.
- Big prints run unattended by necessity. That is only acceptable because someone checks in —
  see the reliability section below for the check-in rhythm.
- **Materials:** PLA and PETG normally; unfilled nylon on the Garolite bed with a mentor.
  **No PA-CF or PC** — no enclosure, no hardened nozzle. Those belong on the
  [Bambu fleet](/3d-printing/bambu-fleet/).

## Software & Workflow

1. Slice in Cura or PrusaSlicer using the stock **CR-6 Max** / **CR-10 Max** machine profile —
   both slicers ship (or import) profiles for these machines. Do not freelance a custom
   machine definition.
2. Apply the wall/infill logic from [Print Settings & Supports](/3d-printing/print-settings/) —
   at this scale every extra wall is hours, so choose deliberately.
3. Add a **brim** for anything tall or with small floor contact. On a day-long print, a corner
   lifting at hour 20 is a catastrophe a brim would have prevented.
4. Save G-code to the printer's memory card, insert, preheat, and start from the machine's
   screen.
5. Watch the entire first layer. At this bed size that takes a while — bring your homework.

## Auto-leveling

Run auto bed leveling **before every large print**. These beds are big enough to drift with
temperature and handling.

1. **Heat-soak first.** Preheat bed (and nozzle) to print temperature and wait ~10 minutes so
   the glass finishes expanding before you probe it.
2. **Clean the nozzle tip** — critical on the CR-6 Max, whose strain gauge probes through the
   nozzle itself. Brush off any blob while warm.
3. Run the leveling cycle from the printer menu (strain-gauge cycle on the CR-6 Max;
   probe cycle on the CR-10 Max).
4. Print a one-layer test patch or skirt and verify squish at the center **and** all four
   corners before committing to a 30-hour job.

## Garolite bed setup for nylon

Unfilled nylon warps off glass but bonds beautifully to **Garolite (G10)** sheet:

1. Clip the Garolite sheet over the glass bed, clips at the edges where the nozzle cannot
   reach.
2. Print nylon directly on the Garolite — no glue needed. Bed around 80–100 °C.
3. Let the bed cool **completely** before removing the part; nylon grips Garolite hard when
   warm and releases as it cools.
4. When adhesion fades, scuff the sheet lightly with a Scotch-Brite pad and wipe with IPA.
5. Dry the nylon first — 12 h at 80 °C, same rule as every nylon in the
   [Filament Guide](/3d-printing/filament-guide/). Wet nylon on a two-day print is two days
   wasted.

Expect honest results, not miracles: on an open-frame machine, tall nylon parts will still
want to warp. Keep nylon parts here low and wide, and take strength-critical nylon to the
Bambus as PA-CF.

## Long-print reliability

Day-long prints fail for boring reasons. Kill the boring reasons:

- **Fresh, dry filament, full spool.** Weigh or eyeball the spool against the slicer's
  filament estimate — with margin. Mid-print spool swaps are a last resort.
- **Thread the runout sensor.** Both machines detect runout and pause — but only if the
  filament actually passes through the sensor.
- **Power-loss resume exists; do not rely on it.** It usually leaves a visible scar line and
  sometimes a failed layer bond at the resume point. It is insurance, not a plan.
- **Glue stick on glass** as a release layer for PETG — big PETG parts can grip carborundum
  glass hard enough to chip it on removal.
- **Check in at the one-hour mark**, then every few hours. Most failures happen in layer one
  or hour one.
- Keep the machine out of A/C drafts; a draft across 400 mm of warm PLA is a warp machine.
- Do not start a multi-day print the night before the machine is needed for something else.

## Cleanup & Maintenance

After your print:

- Let the bed cool, remove the part, scrape any glue residue, and wash the glass with warm
  soapy water when it gets filmy; IPA wipe between prints.
- Unload filament back to a dry box if the machine will sit idle — these printers have no AMS
  babysitting the spool.
- Wipe dust off the Z lead screws if you see debris; report squeaks, clicking, or belt slop to
  a mentor rather than tightening things yourself.

These machines ride the general printer-farm items on the
[maintenance schedule](/maintenance/schedules/) — daily overnight-failure check and weekly
walk-down.
