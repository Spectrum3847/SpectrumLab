Material choice matters more than any slicer setting. A perfectly tuned PLA bracket will still
snap at the first hard hit; a mediocre PETG print of the same part will survive the season.
Pick the material first, then go tune settings on the
[Print Settings & Supports](/3d-printing/print-settings/) page.

## Which material do I use?

Work down this list and stop at the first line that matches your part:

- **Just checking fit, making a jig, or printing something cosmetic?** → **PLA.** Cheapest,
  fastest, easiest. Not for anything that lives on the robot.
- **Going on the robot?** → **PETG.** This is the default answer for brackets, covers, sensor
  mounts, and guards. Tougher than PLA and takes an impact without shattering.
- **Needs to squish, grip, or absorb a hit?** → **TPU.** Intake rollers, bumper pads, grippers,
  gaskets.
- **Highly loaded — gearbox plates, motor mounts, anything you would otherwise machine?** →
  **PA-CF.** Strongest thing we print. Bambu X1-Carbon or H2D only, dried first, no exceptions.
- **Lives next to something hot, or needs max impact resistance at temperature?** → **PC.**
  Enclosed printer required.

When in doubt: prototype in PLA, ship in PETG. Escalate to PA-CF only when PETG has actually
failed or the math says it will.

<SafetyWarning type="warning" title="PLA melts in a hot car">
PLA softens around 55 °C. Parts left in a car in Houston, near the powder-coat curing oven, or
against a hot motor will sag and warp. If a part sees any heat at all, it is not a PLA part.
</SafetyWarning>

## The materials

### PLA — prototypes and jigs

<FilamentCard id="pla" />

PLA is stiff and dimensionally accurate, which makes it great for drill guides and
check-the-CAD prototypes. Its weaknesses are heat and impact: it is the most brittle material
on this list and it creeps under sustained bolt tension. Print it fast, learn from it, then
reprint the final part in something tougher.

### PETG — the on-robot workhorse

<FilamentCard id="petg" />

PETG bends before it breaks, which is exactly what you want from a robot part. It is slightly
harder to print than PLA — it strings, and it welds itself to smooth PEI plates. Always use the
textured plate, and if you see wispy strings, the spool is wet: dry it before blaming the
slicer.

### TPU — flexible parts

<FilamentCard id="tpu" />

TPU prints slow and hates being pushed. Keep speeds down, keep retraction minimal, and feed it
from an external spool holder — standard TPU folds and jams inside the AMS feed path. Only
Bambu's specifically AMS-rated TPU may go through an AMS.

### PA-CF — carbon fiber nylon, the strong stuff

<FilamentCard id="pa-cf" />

PA-CF parts approach machined-plastic strength and shrug off impacts that crack PETG. The cost:
it is expensive, it absolutely must be dry, and the carbon fill sands brass nozzles into
uselessness — hardened nozzle and extruder gears only, which in our lab means the
[Bambu fleet](/3d-printing/bambu-fleet/). A spool left out overnight is a wet spool. Dry it
again before printing.

### PC — polycarbonate for heat and impact

<FilamentCard id="pc" />

PC is the pick when a part needs to survive both heat and abuse. It warps aggressively, so it
only prints in an enclosed machine, and parts should stay in the warm chamber until fully
cooled or the layers will crack. Watch the first layers — PC lifts at corners more than
anything else we run.

## Storage & drying

Filament is a sponge. Wet filament pops, strings, prints weak layers, and in the case of nylon
produces parts with a fraction of their rated strength.

**Storage rules:**

- Open spools live in **dry boxes with desiccant** — not loose on the bench. Reseal partial
  spools in a zip bag with a desiccant pack if a dry box is full.
- Recharge or replace desiccant when the indicator changes color. Saturated desiccant is
  decoration, not protection.
- PA-CF stays in a dry box (or the H2D's AMS HT, which actively dries) at all times. It goes
  straight from drying to the printer.

**Who must be dried, and how much:**

| Material | Drying requirement |
| --- | --- |
| PLA | Usually fine from the box. 50 °C for 8 h only if it strings. |
| PETG | Dry at 65 °C for 8 h if stored open more than ~2 weeks or if stringing. |
| TPU | **Required.** 70 °C for 8–12 h before printing — wet TPU bubbles and strings badly. |
| PA-CF | **Mandatory, every session.** 80 °C for 12 h. Never print a spool that sat out overnight. |
| PC | Dry at 80 °C for 8–12 h; hygroscopic. |

Use a filament dryer or the H2D's AMS HT for drying. Do **not** use the powder-coat curing oven
— its temperature swings will cook a spool into a solid donut.

<SafetyWarning type="tip" title="Symptoms of wet filament">
Popping or hissing at the nozzle, steam wisps, stringing that tuning does not fix, fuzzy matte
surfaces, and weak layer adhesion. If you see two or more of these, stop and dry the spool —
you are wasting printer time otherwise.
</SafetyWarning>

## Buying filament

- **Buy Bambu-brand spools from the Bambu store** (reorder links on each card above). The RFID
  tags auto-configure the AMS and slicer profiles, which removes a whole class of student error.
- Overture PETG and TPU are our approved budget alternates for high-volume printing on the
  [Prusa farm](/3d-printing/prusa-farm/).
- PLA and PETG are bulk staples — keep several spools of each ahead of demand during build
  season. PA-CF and PC are special-order; a mentor approves those purchases.
- New spool arrives? Label it with the date opened, then into a dry box or the AMS.
