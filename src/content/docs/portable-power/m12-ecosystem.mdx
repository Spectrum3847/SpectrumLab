Nearly every cordless tool in the shop runs on one platform: Milwaukee M12. One battery pool, one set of chargers, a dozen bare tools. That is deliberate — any charged battery runs any tool, and a dead battery never strands a job. This page covers the whole fleet except the rivet tools, which have [their own page](/portable-power/m12-riveting/).

## Safety

<SafetyWarning type="critical" title="Battery out before you touch the business end">
Remove the battery before changing bits, blades, wheels, dies, or sanding pads — every time. A trigger bump with your fingers on a blade is how cordless tools bite.
</SafetyWarning>

- Safety goggles on for every cutting, sanding, or drilling operation. See [Gloves & PPE](/safety/gloves-and-ppe/).
- No gloves around rotating bits and blades — they grab.
- Clamp the work. If the part can spin or fly, it will. Never hand-hold small parts against a drill or saw.
- Let blades and wheels stop completely before setting a tool down.
- Carry tools by the body, not the trigger.

## Batteries & Charging Discipline

<LabGalleryImage src="battery-charging-station.jpg" alt="Bench with DeWalt and Milwaukee M12 chargers and batteries" caption="The battery charging bench — dead packs go here, not back in a drawer." />

The fleet only works if the batteries do. Three rules:

1. **Dead batteries go to the charging bench immediately.** Never put a dead pack back in a drawer — the next person finds out mid-hole.
2. **Tools are stored with a charged pack on board.** That is deliberate. Spectrum built the M12 storage box specifically so the fleet could live batteried-up — *"we wanted a way to keep them stored with their batteries installed so they are quicker to grab and return"* ([Lab Upgrades, Jan 2024](https://www.spectrum3847.org/seasons/2024/#2024-01-05-lab-upgrades)). So don't strip a tool at close: **swap the pack, don't remove it**, and send the tired one to the bench.
3. **Battery OUT for every bit, blade, wheel, or pad change.** That is the moment cordless tools bite — not storage. Same rule at the bench and in the pit.
4. **Return tools with a usable pack.** House rule from the drill drawer: the tool goes back with a battery at one bar or better, or you flag it.

Every battery gets charged weekly per the [maintenance schedule](/maintenance/schedules/). If a pack won't hold a charge or gets hot on the charger, label it and report it — don't shuffle it back into the pool.

Every M12 tool has a labeled drawer in one of the two rolling StackTech towers in the machine shop. Tools go back to *their* drawer at close — see [StackTech Tool Towers](/storage/stacktech-drawers/) for the full system.

## Drill/Driver — 3403-20

The M12 FUEL 1/2" drill/driver is the default hole-maker: brushless, ~550 in-lbs, two speed ranges (0–450 and 0–1,700 RPM), 1/2" ratcheting chuck.

<ToolProcurementCard tool="m12-drill" />

**Drilling vs. driving — set the collar:**

- **Drilling:** twist the clutch collar to the **drill-bit symbol**. This bypasses the clutch entirely so the bit never stalls short. Use **speed 2** (high) for small bits in aluminum and plastic, **speed 1** (low) for large bits, hole saws, and step drills.
- **Driving screws:** pick a **numbered clutch setting**. The clutch slips at that torque, which is what saves you from stripping threads or crushing parts. Start low (3–5) for screws into 3D-printed parts and plastic, middle range for wood, and step up only if the screw doesn't seat.
- Driving machine screws into tapped aluminum? Use a low clutch setting and finish by hand with a driver. The clutch is your torque wrench — the drill-symbol setting is not.

Center-punch before drilling metal, use cutting fluid on steel, and back off to clear chips on deep holes. Deburr every hole — see [Cutting & Deburring](/hand-tools/cutting-deburring/).

## Impact Driver — 3453-20

The M12 FUEL 1/4" hex impact driver delivers up to 1,500 in-lbs through rapid rotational hammer blows — 0–3,600 RPM with 4-mode drive control.

<ToolProcurementCard tool="m12-impact" />

**Impact vs. drill — pick right:**

- **Grab the impact** for driving fasteners that need real torque: self-drilling screws into extrusion, lag screws, wood screws, seized bolts. The impacts prevent cam-out and don't twist your wrist.
- **Grab the drill** for making holes and for anything with a torque ceiling — machine screws into tapped aluminum, hardware near printed parts. An impact will happily strip a 10-32 in aluminum before you can release the trigger.
- Use **impact-rated bits only**. Standard hex bits shatter under impact loads.
- Use the drive-mode selector: mode 1 for delicate work, full power only when the fastener needs it. The self-tapping-screw mode stops before you strip the hole it just cut.

## Nibbler — 2476-20

The M12 FUEL 16-gauge nibbler punches a series of crescent-shaped chips to cut sheet without heat, sparks, or cracking. Capacity: 16-ga mild steel, 18-ga stainless, 12-ga aluminum. It is the tool of choice for **sheet polycarbonate and aluminum bellypans** — no melted edges like a jigsaw, no cracked corners like shears.

- Variable-speed dial: slow for tight curves and polycarb, fast for straight runs in aluminum.
- The die rotates tool-free — turn it to cut sideways or toward you in tight spots.
- Leave the protective film on polycarbonate while cutting; mark your line on the film.
- Chips are sharp confetti. Keep the chip bag on, and sweep the bench after — nibbler chips in a forearm is a shop tradition we're ending.
- Deburr the cut edge; nibbled edges are serrated.

## Cut-Off Tool — 2522-20

The M12 FUEL 3" cut-off tool spins a 3" wheel at 20,000 RPM — a compact angle-grinder substitute for trimming bolts, slotting screws, and cutting where nothing else reaches. It takes metal cut-off, diamond tile, and carbide abrasive wheels, with reversible rotation and a tool-free depth-adjust shoe with vacuum port.

- **Sparks.** Clear flammables, know what's behind the workpiece, and never cut toward anyone.
- Guard stays on and between the wheel and your hand. Reverse the rotation instead of repositioning your body into the spark stream.
- Abrasive wheels shatter if twisted in the cut — cut straight, let the wheel do the work.
- Clamp the work. Two hands on the tool.

## Hackzall — 2520-20

The M12 FUEL Hackzall is a one-handed reciprocating saw — brushless, 0–3,000 SPM, 5/8" stroke, with a counterbalance that makes it genuinely controllable one-handed. It is the rough-cut tool: demolition of old robot assemblies, trimming zip-tied bundles of nothing-precious, cutting where you can't bring the work to a bandsaw.

- One hand on the saw, the other hand **nowhere near the blade path** — including behind the workpiece.
- Keep the shoe pressed against the work; a bouncing recip saw skips and grabs.
- Let the blade stop before withdrawing from the cut.
- If the cut needs to be straight or pretty, this is the wrong tool — see the band saw below.

## Compact Band Saw — 2529-20

The M12 FUEL compact band saw cuts up to 2.5" × 2.5" — the go-to for cutting extrusion and tube already mounted on the robot, at roughly 570 SFPM with an included 12/14 TPI blade. For stock at the bench, prefer the stationary saws — see [Bandsaws](/cutting/bandsaws/).

**Portable stock-cutting rules:**

- **Clamp the stock — in a vise or to the bench.** Never hold stock in one hand and the saw in the other. Short pieces especially: if it's too short to clamp with the cut line clear, cut it on a stationary saw.
- Keep **3 teeth in the cut** minimum — thin-wall tube wants the finer pitch.
- Let the saw's own weight feed the cut. Forcing bows the blade and the cut wanders.
- The off-cut drops when the blade breaks through — mind your feet and don't catch it.
- Check blade tracking and condition before cutting; spare blades live in the drawer.

## Jig Saw — 2545-20

The M12 FUEL jig saw is a barrel-grip curve cutter — brushless, 3,000 SPM, 7/8" stroke, tool-free T-shank blade eject, bevel detents at 0/15/30/45°, with an LED and dust blower. Use it for curves and cutouts in plywood and polycarbonate.

- Polycarbonate melts and re-welds behind the blade if you rush: fine-tooth blade, moderate speed, steady feed, film left on.
- Let the shoe sit flat on the work; a rocking jigsaw snaps blades.
- Drill a starter hole for interior cutouts rather than plunge-cutting.
- Blades are cheap — swap a dull one instead of forcing it. Assortment lives in the drawer.

## Crown Stapler — 2447-20

The M12 3/8" crown stapler drives T50-style flat crown staples, 1/4" to 9/16" leg. It's the fast way to attach bumper fabric, banners, and fabric to plywood.

- **Treat it like a nail gun**, because it is one. Never point it at anyone, never hold work directly behind the staple point.
- Keep it in **sequential actuation** (safety tip pressed, then trigger). Contact-actuation bump-firing is off-limits for bumper day chaos.
- Staple boxes live in the same drawer as the tool.

## Rotary Tool — 2525-20

The M12 brushless rotary tool runs 5,000–27,500 RPM across 12 speed settings with a 1/8" collet (1/32", 1/16", and 3/32" collets fit too). At 0.8 lb it reaches where nothing else does: deburring inside tube, grinding a weld nub, engraving, cutting one seized screw in place.

- Fiberglass-reinforced cut-off discs still shatter — goggles always, and keep the disc square to the cut.
- Match speed to the accessory's rating; small mounted stones don't want full RPM.
- Grip close to the collet like a fat pencil for control.

## Detail Sander — 2531-20

The M12 FUEL orbital detail sander has a pointed pad for corners, a tight 1.5 mm orbit, and a counterweight that kills most of the buzz. Use it for easing plywood edges, finishing bumper backing, and smoothing 3D-printed parts before paint.

- Let the pad float — pressing harder just burns paper and swirls the finish.
- Work through grits; don't ask 80-grit to be a finish pass.
- Sanding dust plus the shop vac beats sanding dust plus your lungs — see the [DeWalt vacuum](/portable-power/dewalt-and-pneumatics/) and wear a dust mask for long sessions.

## Band File — the pit's secret weapon

The M12 band file is a narrow abrasive belt on a long arm, and it does one thing nothing else in the
shop can: **sand a part that is still bolted to the robot.** Deburring a cut you just made in place,
cleaning up a weld nub inside a frame rail, easing an edge in a pocket a belt sander will never
reach — all of it, without disassembly. In the pit it is faster than a file and far faster than
taking the mechanism apart.

- Two hands, and know where the belt exits — it throws grit along its own axis.
- Light pressure. The belt is thin; leaning on it snaps belts and gouges the part.
- Glasses on, and mind that the part gets hot fast because the contact patch is tiny.

## Subcompact Drill & Driver

Alongside the FUEL tools the shop keeps a couple of **subcompact** M12 drills and impact drivers.
They are shorter nose-to-tail than the FUEL bodies, which makes them the right pick for small hands
and for driving inside a mechanism where the full-size tool won't fit square. They have enough
torque for nearly every robot task — reach for a FUEL drill only when you actually need the grunt.

## Installation Driver, Oscillating Tool & Die Grinder

Three more that live in the towers and get forgotten:

- **Installation driver** — a right-angle/offset driver for fasteners a straight tool can't line up
  on.
- **Oscillating multi-tool** — flush-cutting and plunge-cutting where a rotating blade can't go,
  including trimming something already installed.
- **Die grinder** — burrs and stones for shaping and grinding in tight spots. Same rules as the
  rotary tool: match the accessory's rated speed, goggles always.

Each needs a mentor walk-through before first solo use, same as the saws.

## Cleanup & Maintenance

- Tool back in its labeled drawer with a **charged pack on it**, drawer closed. Battery out only for a bit/blade change or if the tool is going in for service.
- Dead packs to the charging bench; charged packs back to the battery pool.
- Blow or brush chips out of vents and blade guards before the tool goes away.
- Dull blades and worn wheels get replaced now, not discovered later — restock from the drawer spares and note anything low on the [consumables ledger](/inventory/consumables/).
- Weekly battery charging and fleet checks are on the [maintenance schedule](/maintenance/schedules/).
