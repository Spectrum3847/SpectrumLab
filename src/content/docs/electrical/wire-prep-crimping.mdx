A robot is only as reliable as its worst <Term id="crimp">crimp</Term>. Robots vibrate, and solder joints crack under vibration — which is why FRC wiring is crimped, not soldered, everywhere it matters. This page covers the tools and the standards: strip clean, crimp with the right tool, and tug-test every single connection.

The crimpers, strippers, and terminal assortments live in the electrical storage — the labeled Electrical drawer in the Durham cabinet and THE Electrical Case on the HDX rack.

<SafetyWarning type="critical" title="Dead robot before live hands">
Disconnect the battery before cutting, stripping, or crimping anything on the robot. A wrench or wire strand across a live 12V battery circuit welds itself in place and starts fires. Battery out, main breaker off, then work.
</SafetyWarning>

## Wire Strippers

Two strippers, two jobs:

- **Standard strippers** handle signal and power wiring roughly 10–22 <Term id="awg">AWG</Term>. Use the labeled slot that matches your wire gauge — a smaller slot nicks strands, and every nicked strand is capacity lost and a future break point.
- **Large-gauge strippers** handle the fat stuff: 6 AWG battery leads and main power runs. Don't chew 6 AWG with the small strippers; the insulation tears and the strands splay.

**Good strip checklist:** insulation cut clean, no nicked or missing strands, strip length matched to the terminal barrel (strands should fill the barrel and stop — no bare wire showing behind it, no insulation inside it). Don't twist stranded wire before crimping into terminals; leave the strands as they lay.

## Ratcheting Crimpers

The ratcheting crimpers are the workhorses for insulated and non-insulated terminals — ring lugs, spade terminals, butt splices, <Term id="ferrule">ferrules</Term>. The ratchet mechanism is the point: **it will not release until the crimp is fully formed**, so every crimp gets full, repeatable pressure.

- **Insulated terminals** follow the universal color code: **red = 22–16 AWG, blue = 16–14 AWG, yellow = 12–10 AWG**. Match the wire, the terminal, and the color-marked die position.
- **Non-insulated terminals** use the non-insulated die profile — it folds the barrel instead of compressing an insulation sleeve. Using the insulated die on a bare terminal makes a loose, ugly crimp.
- Seat the terminal in the die, insert the stripped wire until strands are fully in the barrel, squeeze through the full ratchet cycle.
- **Tug test every crimp.** Pull hard — harder than feels polite. If it moves, cut it off and do it again. A crimp that fails in your hand was going to fail in a match.

## Anderson Powerpole Connectors

<Term id="powerpole">Powerpoles</Term> are the team standard for connectorized power runs — genderless, polarized by assembly, and rebuildable.

1. **Crimp the contact** with the Powerpole crimper (or the correct die), stripped wire fully in the contact barrel. No strands outside, no insulation inside.
2. **Orient the contact** before insertion: the contact's rolled tongue rides *up* against the housing's spring — look into the housing, find the spring, and align the contact so its flat side faces it per the housing's mold marks.
3. **Push the contact in until it clicks** past the spring.
4. **Click retention test:** tug the wire firmly backward. A properly seated contact does not come out. If it slides out, it was never locked — reinsert, listen for the click, test again.
5. **Assemble housings** in the team-standard orientation (red/black paired per our existing harnesses — match an existing known-good pair, don't improvise), and add the roll pin if the pair should stay mated as a set.

## Hydraulic Crimper — Battery Lugs

Battery leads and main power cables (6 AWG) get lugs set with the hydraulic crimper. This is the one crimp on the robot that carries every amp the battery can deliver, so it's a process, not a squeeze:

1. Strip the 6 AWG to the lug barrel depth with the large-gauge strippers.
2. **Slide the heat shrink on first.** Adhesive-lined, correct diameter. Everyone forgets once.
3. Select the die that matches the lug size (dies are stamped), seat the lug, insert the wire fully.
4. Pump the hydraulic crimper until the dies bottom out completely. For long barrels, crimp twice along the barrel.
5. **Tug test** — brace and pull with real force. The lug should feel like part of the wire.
6. Slide the heat shrink over the barrel and shrink it with the [heat gun](/portable-power/dewalt-and-pneumatics/#20v-heat-gun--dce530) on low until it's tight and the adhesive just beads at the edge. The shrink covers all exposed barrel — no bare copper.
7. Inspect: no loose strands at the barrel mouth, no cracks in the crimp, insulation undamaged.

## Bench-Mount Lug Crimper — iCrimp IWS-0801DTS

New in September 2026. A bench-mounted hex crimper for battery-cable lugs, bought to sit alongside the hydraulic crimper rather than replace it.

The difference that matters: it has **five fixed die cavities — 8, 6, 4, 2, and 1 AWG — stamped into the head**, so there is no die to select, swap, or lose. You pick the cavity, you crimp. That makes it the faster and harder-to-get-wrong choice for a batch of same-size lugs, which is most of what battery-lead season looks like. It bolts down to the bench, so it also frees both hands for the cable.

Two things it is *not*: it stops at 1 AWG, so anything larger stays on the hydraulic crimper; and its hex profile is for **non-insulated copper lugs**, not insulated terminals — those still belong in the ratcheting crimpers.

It has a **built-in cable cutter** in the head. Use it for the cable, not for anything else — nicking the shear on a bolt or a zip tie ruins the one cut in the shop that leaves a square, unsplayed 6 AWG end.

The crimping process is otherwise the same as the hydraulic procedure above, and every step of it still applies: heat shrink on **first**, strands fully in the barrel, close the handle through its full stroke until the dies bottom out, tug test hard, then shrink and inspect.

<SafetyWarning type="warning" title="Bolt it down before the first crimp">
This tool is designed to be fixed to a bench, and the handle is long enough to take real force. Crimping it loose or hand-held lets it skid or tip mid-stroke, which gives a half-formed crimp at best. If it isn't bolted down, don't use it — say something instead.
</SafetyWarning>

## FRC Wire Gauge Rules

The breaker protects the **wire**, not the device — so wire gauge is dictated by the breaker feeding the circuit. The big ones to memorize:

| Circuit breaker | Minimum wire size |
| --- | --- |
| 40A | 12 AWG |
| 30A | 14 AWG |
| 20A | 18 AWG |

- Battery leads and the main power path run **6 AWG** through the 120A main breaker.
- Motors on 40A breakers (drivetrain, most mechanisms) get 12 AWG — no exceptions, no "it was in the bin."
- Smaller control and signal circuits have their own table entries — **check the wiring table in the current game manual** before pulling anything unusual, since exact rules are re-published every season.
- When in doubt, go one gauge heavier. Copper is cheaper than a rewire at competition.
