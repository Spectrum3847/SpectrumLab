The electronics bench is where custom boards get built, mystery boards get diagnosed, and sensor wiring gets repaired. Robot power wiring is crimped, not soldered ([here's why](/electrical/wire-prep-crimping/)) — the bench is for everything else: signal wiring, connectors, LED strips, and board-level work.

<SafetyWarning type="warning" title="The hot end is invisible">
A soldering iron at 350 °C looks identical to a cold one. Always rack the iron in its stand, never lay it on the bench, and treat every iron as hot until you've checked its screen. Pass irons handle-first.
</SafetyWarning>

**Power:** the PINECIL is only as strong as its supply. Use a **USB-C PD brick rated 60W or better** (or the barrel supply) — a phone charger will boot the iron but starve it, and it will lose the fight against a ground plane. If the iron sags in temperature mid-joint, suspect the supply first.

**Temperature:** set the lowest temperature that gets the job done fast.

- **Leaded solder (63/37, 60/40):** 320–350 °C
- **Lead-free solder:** 350–380 °C
- Big joints (ground pours, thick wire) want the top of the range, not more time grinding at the joint.

**Tip care:**

- **<Term id="tinning">Tin</Term> the tip** before you start and before the iron goes to sleep — a thin silver coat of fresh solder. A black, oxidized tip transfers no heat.
- Clean on **brass wool**, not a wet sponge — the sponge's thermal shock shortens tip life. Wipe, re-tin, solder.
- IronOS sleeps the iron when it sits still and wakes it when you pick it up. Don't fight it; it's saving your tips.

## Soldering Technique

One rule above all: **heat the joint, not the solder.**

1. Slide any heat shrink on *before* joining — yes, check now.
2. Touch the iron so it contacts **both** the pad and the lead (or both wires). Give it a second.
3. Feed solder into the **joint**, on the side away from the iron. When the joint is hot enough, solder flows toward the heat and wets everything it touches.
4. Remove solder, then iron. Total contact: 2–3 seconds for typical joints. Hold the parts dead still while the solder freezes.
5. Read the joint: a good one is shiny (leaded) with a smooth concave fillet, like a tiny volcano. A dull ball sitting *on top* of the wire is a <Term id="cold-joint">cold joint</Term> — reheat it properly, don't blob more solder on it.

Melting solder on the iron and carrying it to a cold joint makes a connection that looks fine and fails in the pit. Don't.

## Benchtop DC Power Supply

The bench supply powers boards and mechanisms for testing without risking a robot battery or a control system.

<SafetyWarning type="critical" title="Set the current limit BEFORE powering an unknown board">
Before connecting anything you didn't personally verify: set the voltage, short the leads (or use the supply's setting mode) and dial the <Term id="current-limit">current limit</Term> down to ~100 mA, THEN connect the board. If the supply immediately hits constant-current mode, the board has a short — and you just found it for free instead of with smoke.
</SafetyWarning>

Workflow for any device under test:

1. Set the **voltage** to the device's rated input. Verify on the display before connecting.
2. Set the **current limit** just above the expected draw — start low (100 mA for logic boards) and raise it deliberately as the device proves itself.
3. Connect with clip leads, mind the polarity, and watch the current readout at power-on. Known current draw is your first diagnostic: zero means no connection, limit means short, expected means proceed.
4. Power off at the supply before changing any connection.

## Rigol DS1052E Oscilloscope

The Rigol DS1052E is the bench scope: 50 MHz, 2 channels, 1 GSa/s. It's an older workhorse, and it does everything a robot signal needs.

<ToolProcurementCard tool="rigol-ds1052e" />

**Probe basics:**

- Use probes at **10x** for almost everything — higher input impedance, less circuit loading, full voltage range. Set the switch on the probe *and* tell the scope: Channel menu → Probe → 10x. If your measured 5V rail reads 0.5V, the scope thinks the probe is 1x.
- Clip the probe's ground lead to circuit ground, close to the measurement point.
- Before trusting a probe, check it on the scope's calibration terminal (square wave) and trim the compensation screw until the corners are square.

**Trigger basics:** a stable picture means the trigger is right.

- **Edge trigger** on the channel you're watching; set the trigger **level** to the middle of the signal.
- **Auto** sweep mode to browse and find signals; **Normal** when you want the screen to update only when the real event happens.
- Scrolling or shimmering waveform? Fix the trigger level first.

**Measuring PWM** (the bread and butter — motor controller and servo signals):

1. Probe the signal wire at 10x, ground clip to signal ground.
2. Edge trigger at ~50% of the pulse height.
3. Use the **Measure** menu: frequency, positive pulse width, and +duty cycle read out directly.
4. Sanity checks: a hobby-servo-style signal sits near 50–330 Hz with ~1–2 ms pulses; addressable LED data looks like garbage at slow timebases — zoom in.

**Never probe mains wall power with this scope.** It's a low-voltage bench instrument; robot electronics only.

## Ventilation & Fumes

Solder smoke is vaporized <Term id="flux">flux</Term>, and it goes straight up into the face of whoever's leaning over the joint.

- Run the fume fan/extractor at the bench, or position a fan to pull smoke away from your face.
- The lead in leaded solder doesn't vaporize at iron temperatures, but it does stay on your hands — **wash your hands after soldering**, especially before food.
- Keep the bench window/door airflow in mind for long sessions.

## Cleanup

- Tin the tip, then sleep/unplug the iron. Let it cool in the stand before it's stored.
- Power supply off, leads coiled and hung.
- Probes back with the scope — ground clips attached, not orphaned in a drawer.
- Solder spatter and clipped leads swept into the trash, not left for elbows.
- Wash your hands.
